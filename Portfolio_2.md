# Business Process Modelling #

**Introduction to Business Process Modelling**

Business process modelling visually shows how activities, decisions, information and responsibilities come together in a process. It helps organisations see how things currently work explain how work is done and create ways of doing things. This e-portfolio shares my learning about BPMN, SIPOC different modelling levels and process simulation.

**BPMN and AS-IS / TO-BE Process Modelling**

Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) is a way to show processes using symbols for activities, events, decisions and flows. The AS-IS model shows how a process works now. The TO-BE model shows how it should work after improvements. Using the BPMN symbols consistently matters because clear models help everyone involved understand the process better. Studies have found that using BPMN elements like gateways—makes models easier to read and understand (Richter, Fantinato & Thom 2026 p. 571).
From this artefact I learned that process modelling is not just drawing lines and boxes. Looking at both AS-IS and TO-BE models side by side makes changes easier to see before they are put into action. This shows my understanding because I can link process modelling, with communication and redesign. It is evidence of my grasp of BPM because a well-made model supports better choices when improving processes.

**SIPOC for High-Level Process Modelling**

A specific approach to process management in the industry

SIPOC is a high-level process mapping technique that identifies Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs and Customers. SIPOC is especially helpful at the start of process modelling because SIPOC sets the process boundary before detailed modelling begins. SIPOC gives a view of the main parts needed to deliver an outcome. Research in manufacturing says that SIPOC is a useful way to find essential process elements from suppliers to customers (Bogorin-Predescu & Titu 2025 p. 45).

I learned that effective modelling does not always begin with a diagram. Building my SIPOC model helped me identify the scope, inputs, outputs and stakeholders of a process first. This shows my Week 4 learning through work instead of just describing the idea. This is evidence because I can use SIPOC to set a clear foundation before I create a more detailed process model.

**Process Model Levels and Business Process Architecture**

 A Knowledge-Life Cycle Driven Riva Business Process Architecture

Business processes can be drawn at different levels depending on what the purpose is and who will look at them. Enterprise models give a high‑level view. Business‑level models show end‑to‑end processes. Workflow models describe day‑to‑day activities. Task‑level models give step‑by‑step details of how each activity's carried out. Process architecture links all these views by showing how critical organisational processes relate to one another. Sabri says that process architecture gives a systematic overview that connects key processes inside an organisational setting (Sabri 2025 p. 374).

I learned that one process model cannot give the level of information for every user. Senior managers may need an enterprise‑level view while operational employees need workflow or task details. This artefact shows my Week 5 learning because artefact allows me to see the differences between modelling levels and understand their purposes. This artefact provides evidence because choosing the right level makes process information easier to share, analyse and use.

**Process Simulation and Performance Evaluation**

Evolution of BPMN and Simulation Integration: Trends Challenges and Future Directions

Process simulation lets organisations test how a process model might perform under conditions before any changes are made. Variables such as activity duration, workload and resource availability can be changed to compare process designs. Simulation therefore goes beyond visual diagrams by giving data about expected performance. Research into BPMN and simulation shows that simulation can help with resource management, performance optimisation and decision‑making (Cimino et al. 2025 Pp. 3235–3236).

I learned that a process model should not be judged successful only because the diagram looks logical. Process model therefore requires testing before any resources are committed. This links my Week 5 and Week 6 learning with process measures such, as time, cost, capacity and quality. This artefact gives evidence of my BPM understanding because simulation can uncover possible bottlenecks and lower the risk of putting in an ineffective process design.
